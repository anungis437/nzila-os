# Public-Service Conversation Guide

*For Aubert and Michel to use on introductory calls. The goal of a first conversation
is feedback and fit, not a sale.*

## Opening

- Thank them for their time and be explicit that this is not a sales call.
- Frame it plainly: "We are developing a public-service continuity framing called
  CIVIC by Nzila, and we are looking for feedback on whether it is useful and where
  it would fit institutionally."
- Make it easy to end early: "If this isn't relevant to your work, that's useful to
  know too."

## What not to pitch

Do not, in a first conversation, offer or reference:

- pricing
- a statement of work (SOW)
- a pilot as an ask ("start pilot")
- a product demo ("book a demo")
- a platform trial ("try the platform")
- a paid assessment ("buy assessment")
- UnionEyes as the public-service offer ("UnionEyes for government")

These belong to a later stage, if ever. The first conversation earns the right to a
second one.

## Core thesis

Public institutions already hold policy commitments, service standards, values
statements, modernization plans, and accountability obligations. The operating risk
is whether those commitments stay traceable — through workflows, ownership, evidence
quality, institutional memory, and recourse — as workforce transition and modernization
accelerate. CIVIC focuses on that operating layer between policy commitment and
institutional action.

## Questions to ask

- Where do you already feel continuity or institutional-memory pressure?
- As experienced staff transition, what knowledge is hardest to preserve?
- Where does evidence for accountability or equity implementation live today, and how
  fragmented is it?
- As you modernize or adopt AI, how do you confirm a change is implementation-safe and
  reviewable before it becomes the default?
- What would make an evidence brief genuinely useful to an executive in your context?

## Buyer-routing questions

To understand where this would sit institutionally (without pitching):

- Who owns service continuity and operational risk in your organization?
- Who owns modernization / responsible-AI decisions?
- Who owns equity implementation and its evidence?
- Where does internal audit or enterprise risk intersect with this?
- Is this a CIO, COO, deputy-head, or enterprise-risk conversation in your world?

## Sensitivity boundaries

- Do not claim to detect wrongdoing, discrimination, or racism.
- Do not offer legal, HR, or equity determinations.
- Do not imply autonomous decision-making. Human review is authoritative.
- Be explicit about uncertainty and evidence confidence.
- Treat privacy and data minimization as first-class, not afterthoughts.

## How to explain ABR visibility safely

If anti-racism / ABR comes up:

- Frame it strictly as a **visibility and traceability lens** — helping trace whether
  equity and anti-racism commitments remain visible in workflows, ownership, evidence,
  and recourse.
- Be explicit about what it is **not**: it is not a racism detector, it does not produce
  automated findings of discrimination, and it does not score people or groups.
- The value is helping human reviewers see where commitments may be losing traceability,
  not rendering judgments.

## How to mention CourtLens only if asked

- Do not lead with CourtLens. It is not the public-service pitch.
- If asked about access-to-justice work, describe CourtLens only as an **adjacent proof
  point** that reflects the same evidence discipline, then return to the public-service
  continuity framing.

## Closing ask

- Ask for candid feedback: is the framing useful, and where would it fit?
- If they are open to it, ask who else it would be worth speaking with — for example a
  colleague in continuity/operations, modernization/responsible-AI, internal audit or
  enterprise risk, or equity implementation.
- Offer to **request a briefing** follow-up: a short, tailored continuity brief for their
  context, on their terms.
