# The CLEAR Method

## The evidence discipline behind CIVIC by Nzila

> **Alignment (canonical).** CLEAR is the **public-service articulation of the OCI evidence
> discipline**. Its five pillars map to OCI's scoring dimensions, evidence ladder, obligation
> taxonomy, and explainability contract — see the
> [CIVIC ↔ OCI alignment Rosetta table](../CIVIC_OCI_ALIGNMENT.md#4-rosetta-table-how-the-five-pillars-line-up).
> CLEAR does not introduce new scores, bands, or rankings; it is how the OCI evidence work
> reads to a public-service leader.

CLEAR is the method that makes CIVIC by Nzila credible. Where CIVIC is the public-service
continuity front door, CLEAR is the repeatable evidence discipline that turns scattered,
tacit, and fragmented institutional signals into decision-grade material a human leader can
act on — with confidence and uncertainty stated plainly.

CLEAR stands for **Continuity, Legitimacy, Evidence, Accountability, and Readiness.**

This document is the canonical definition of the method. It is written for public-service
leaders, and it is deliberately not a product, an assessment, a scorecard, or a procurement
offer. It is a way of working that supports human judgment; it does not replace it.

---

## 1. What CLEAR is

CLEAR is a repeatable evidence discipline for public-service continuity. It gives leaders a
consistent way to ask a hard institutional question, gather what can actually be observed,
judge how strong that evidence is, and produce a human-reviewed brief that names both what is
known and what is still uncertain.

CLEAR is used to examine five things that tend to weaken quietly during workforce transition
and modernization:

- **Institutional memory** — the knowledge, service logic, and decision context that live in
  people and practice rather than in durable records.
- **Governance evidence** — the records of decisions, rationale, controls, and recourse that
  accountability depends on.
- **Responsible modernization** — whether a change is safe, reviewable, and understood before
  it becomes the operating default.
- **Equity, accessibility, Official Languages, and GBA Plus implementation traceability** —
  whether stated equity, accessibility, official-language, and GBA Plus commitments remain visible
  in how work is actually done.
- **Service continuity** — whether critical services can be sustained, explained, and improved
  through change.

CLEAR treats *uncertainty as information*, not as failure. A CLEAR output is honest about the
limits of its own evidence, because a brief that overstates its confidence is more dangerous
to an institution than one that admits what it does not yet know.

---

## 2. What CLEAR is not

CLEAR's boundaries are part of the method, not a disclaimer bolted onto it.

- CLEAR does not provide legal advice.
- CLEAR does not make HR, equity, employment, or disciplinary decisions.
- CLEAR does not produce automated findings of racism, discrimination, wrongdoing, or
  misconduct.
- CLEAR does not replace internal audit, legal review, equity offices, HR, or executive
  accountability.
- CLEAR does not score, rank, or rate people, groups, or institutions.
- CLEAR does not automate accountability — accountability stays with named human owners.

What CLEAR does is narrower and more honest: it supports human review by organizing evidence,
uncertainty, and traceability so that the people who are accountable can see more clearly and
decide for themselves.

---

## 3. The five CLEAR pillars

CLEAR examines an institutional question through five pillars. Each pillar asks a different
question about whether the institution can sustain, explain, and improve what it does.

| Pillar | Core question |
| --- | --- |
| **Continuity** | Will critical institutional knowledge and service logic survive change? |
| **Legitimacy** | Is the way this is done authorized, fair, and defensible? |
| **Evidence** | Can the institution show its work, not just assert it? |
| **Accountability** | Is it clear who owns the decision and how it can be reviewed? |
| **Readiness** | Is the institution prepared to modernize without losing control? |

The pillars are examined together. A weakness in one often explains fragility in another —
thin evidence usually accompanies unclear accountability, and low readiness usually accompanies
concentrated, undocumented continuity risk.

---

## 4. Observable evidence for each pillar

A method is only as serious as what it is willing to count as evidence. For each pillar, CLEAR
defines a plain-language meaning, why it matters in the public service, what can actually be
observed, the warning signs of weakness, and the questions to ask.

### Continuity

- **Plain-language meaning:** Whether critical institutional knowledge, service logic,
  ownership, and decision context survive workforce, system, and operating change.
- **Public-service relevance:** As experienced staff transition, much of what keeps a program
  compliant and fair is undocumented and leaves with them.
- **Observable evidence:**
  - Documented service logic and process ownership (not only in people's heads).
  - Succession and knowledge-transfer records for critical roles.
  - Runbooks, decision logs, and precedent records that a successor could actually use.
  - Cross-training or more than one person who understands a critical process.
- **Warning signs:**
  - A critical process understood by only one or two people.
  - Onboarding that depends on mentorship with no durable record behind it.
  - Institutional memory that exists mainly in inboxes and individual judgment.
- **Questions to ask:**
  - If this person left tomorrow, what would the institution no longer be able to explain?
  - Where is service logic written down in a form a successor could use?

### Legitimacy

> **Scope note:** Legitimacy does not mean CLEAR decides whether a policy choice is legitimate.
> It means the evidence package checks whether the *operating practice* appears traceable to
> authority, policy, procedural fairness, accessibility, official-language obligations, and review expectations —
> for human consideration, never as a verdict on a government decision.

- **Plain-language meaning:** Whether the way a service or decision is carried out appears
  traceable to authority, consistent with policy and law, procedurally fair, and defensible.
- **Public-service relevance:** Legitimacy is what lets an institution stand behind a practice
  publicly and withstand scrutiny, and it anchors public confidence and values and ethics.
- **Observable evidence (Canadian dimensions):**
  - **Statutory / policy authority** — a clear line from legislation or policy to the operating
    practice.
  - **Mandate fit** — the practice sits within the institution's mandate.
  - **Procedural fairness** — consistent application of rules with documented, principled
    exceptions.
  - **Official languages** — obligations reflected where the practice is client-facing.
  - **Accessibility** — barrier considerations reflected in how the service actually runs.
  - **GBA Plus / differential impact** — whether differential impacts have been considered.
  - **Reconciliation / Indigenous considerations** — where relevant to the practice.
  - **Public confidence / values and ethics** — transparency about how and why decisions are made.
- **Warning signs:**
  - Practice that has drifted from its original policy or legislative basis.
  - Undocumented workarounds that quietly become the real rule.
  - Inconsistent treatment of similar cases with no recorded rationale.
  - Accessibility, official-language, or GBA Plus considerations absent from the operating layer.
- **Questions to ask:**
  - What authorizes this practice, and can we trace the line from authority to operation?
  - Where has practice drifted from what policy actually says?
  - Are official-language, accessibility, and GBA Plus expectations visible in how it runs?

### Evidence

- **Plain-language meaning:** Whether the institution can show its work — produce the records,
  rationale, and traceability behind what it does — rather than only assert it.
- **Public-service relevance:** Accountability and equity commitments are tested during change,
  which is exactly when fragmented evidence tends to fall apart.
- **Observable evidence:**
  - Decision records with rationale, not just outcomes.
  - Traceability from commitment to implementation to review.
  - Records held in durable, findable systems rather than scattered across inboxes.
  - Data and evidence that can survive a staff or system transition.
- **Warning signs:**
  - Evidence spread across HR, operations, audit, complaints, and personal files with no
    connective thread.
  - Commitments that are strongly stated but weakly evidenced.
  - Records that cannot be found when a transition or review actually needs them.
- **Questions to ask:**
  - If we had to show our work on this tomorrow, could we — and where is it?
  - How fragmented is the evidence, and would it survive the people who hold it leaving?

### Accountability

- **Plain-language meaning:** Whether it is clear who owns a decision, how it was reached, and
  how it can be questioned and corrected.
- **Public-service relevance:** Accountability that cannot survive workforce transition and
  system change is accountability in name only.
- **Observable evidence:**
  - A named decision owner for the process or commitment.
  - Documented rationale for how the decision was reached.
  - A defined escalation pathway.
  - A review record showing the decision was revisited when it should be.
  - A control or audit trail.
  - A recourse pathway for people affected by the decision.
- **Warning signs:**
  - Ownership that is diffuse, shared into invisibility, or unassigned.
  - Decisions with no recorded rationale and no way to trace who made them.
  - No route for affected people to seek review or recourse.
- **Questions to ask:**
  - Who owns this decision, and how would someone challenge or review it?
  - Would the accountability for this still hold if the current owner moved on?

### Readiness

- **Plain-language meaning:** Whether the institution is prepared to modernize — including
  using AI where appropriate — without losing continuity, control, or reviewability.
- **Public-service relevance:** Modernization can encode today's fragile or unexamined
  assumptions into tomorrow's automated defaults, faster and harder to question.
- **Observable evidence:**
  - A way to confirm a change is safe and reviewable before it becomes the default.
  - Human review points designed into modernization, not added afterward.
  - Documented assumptions behind an automated or redesigned workflow.
  - A rollback or correction path when a change turns out to be wrong.
- **Warning signs:**
  - A modernization decision about to become a default before it has been reviewed.
  - Automated workflows whose underlying assumptions no one can articulate.
  - No human checkpoint between a proposed change and its becoming operational.
- **Questions to ask:**
  - How do we confirm a change is safe and reviewable before it becomes the default?
  - If an automated change is wrong, how quickly can a human see it and correct it?

## 5. Evidence confidence levels

Every CLEAR finding is labelled with an explicit confidence level. This is central to the
method: it keeps the institution honest about the difference between what it knows and what it
believes.

- **High confidence** — The evidence is durable, corroborated across more than one source, and
  would survive a staff or system transition. A leader can rely on it directly.
- **Moderate confidence** — The evidence is real but partial: single-source, dated, or
  dependent on individual knowledge. Usable, but with stated caveats.
- **Low confidence** — The signal is present but weakly evidenced. It points to something worth
  attention, not to a conclusion.
- **Insufficient evidence** — There is not enough to say. The honest output is to name the gap
  and the evidence that would be needed to close it.

**Low confidence is not failure.** It is a finding in its own right: it means the institution
needs better evidence before it makes a decision, and it tells leaders exactly where to invest
in that evidence. A CLEAR brief that surfaces uncertainty early is doing its job.

## 6. CLEAR workflow

CLEAR is a sequence, not a single assessment. The workflow is designed to be repeatable across
different institutional questions.

1. **Define the institutional question.** State the specific continuity, memory, evidence,
   accountability, or modernization question the institution actually needs answered.
2. **Map the operating context.** Understand how the service, decision, or commitment actually
   works today — including the informal steps and undocumented judgment.
3. **Identify evidence sources.** Locate where relevant evidence lives, across systems, records,
   and people, and note where it is fragmented.
4. **Classify evidence strength.** Apply the confidence levels to what was found, distinguishing
   durable evidence from single-source or tacit knowledge.
5. **Surface continuity and accountability gaps.** Name where memory is concentrated, evidence
   is thin, ownership is unclear, or a change is about to become an unreviewed default.
6. **Produce a human-reviewed brief.** Bring the findings to the accountable humans as
   decision-grade material — never as an automated verdict.
7. **Record uncertainty and next evidence needed.** Document what remains uncertain and the
   specific evidence that would raise confidence, so the institution knows where to invest next.

The workflow always ends with humans deciding. CLEAR organizes the material; accountable people
draw the conclusions.

---

## 7. Outputs CLEAR can produce

CLEAR produces concrete, human-reviewed artifacts. These are working outputs of the method, not
commercial deliverables.

- **Public-service continuity brief** — an executive-readable summary of where continuity,
  memory, and service logic are under pressure, with confidence stated.
- **Evidence confidence note** — a plain statement of how strong the evidence is behind a given
  commitment or claim, and what would strengthen it.
- **Implementation traceability map** — a view of how a commitment travels from policy through
  operation to review, and where the thread breaks.
- **Modernization readiness summary** — a readable account of whether a change is safe and
  reviewable before it becomes the operating default.
- **Human-review risk register** — a record of the continuity and accountability signals that
  warrant human attention, each with its confidence level and next evidence need.

Every output is explicitly framed for human review, carries its uncertainty with it, and defers
the decision to the accountable people.

---

## 8. Human-review and accountability boundaries

These boundaries are non-negotiable and are embedded in the method itself.

- Human review remains authoritative at every step.
- CLEAR does not provide legal advice.
- CLEAR does not make HR, equity, employment, or disciplinary decisions.
- CLEAR does not produce automated findings of racism, discrimination, wrongdoing, or
  misconduct.
- CLEAR does not replace internal audit, legal review, equity offices, HR, or executive
  accountability.
- CLEAR supports human review by organizing evidence, uncertainty, and traceability.

On equity specifically: CLEAR treats equity and anti-racism only as a matter of **visibility and
traceability** — helping human decision-makers see whether commitments remain visible in the
operating layer. It does not detect racism, render verdicts, or score people or groups. Privacy
and data minimization are in scope from the start.

## 9. Public-service use cases

CLEAR is designed for the pressures public institutions face together during transition and
modernization. Illustrative uses include:

- **Workforce transition.** Surfacing where critical institutional knowledge is concentrated in
  too few people before those people move on.
- **Accountability under change.** Checking whether the evidence behind a decision or commitment
  could survive a reorganization or a system replacement.
- **Responsible modernization.** Confirming that a redesigned or automated workflow is safe and
  reviewable before it becomes the default.
- **Equity implementation.** Making visible whether a stated equity or anti-racism commitment
  still shows up in how the work is actually done.
- **Service continuity.** Understanding whether a critical service can be sustained, explained,
  and improved through change rather than merely kept running.

In each case, the output is decision-grade material for the accountable humans — not a
conclusion drawn on their behalf.

## 10. Relationship to CIVIC and SAGE

CLEAR sits within a deliberately simple structure.

- **CIVIC** is the public-service continuity front door — the framing that helps leaders see
  where continuity, memory, evidence, accountability, and modernization readiness are under
  pressure.
- **CLEAR** is the evidence discipline behind CIVIC — the repeatable method that turns
  fragmented signals into decision-grade, human-reviewed material with confidence and
  uncertainty stated plainly.
- **SAGE** is a future-facing direction only: a possible workspace for repeatable service
  assurance, governance, and evidence. It is named here to show where the thinking could go, not
  to describe anything available today.

CIVIC opens the conversation. CLEAR is what makes that conversation credible. SAGE is a horizon,
not a current offer.

## 11. Canadian public-sector alignment

CLEAR is designed to sit comfortably inside the operating reality of the Government of Canada. It
does not certify compliance and it does not replace any authoritative departmental process. What
it does is help institutions organize evidence in a way that supports their existing obligations
and surfaces where a departmental review may be needed.

The table below maps CLEAR to Canadian public-sector instruments and expectations. The language is
deliberately careful: CLEAR *supports*, *helps surface*, and *helps organize evidence for* these
obligations — it does not discharge them.

> **Applicability note.** CLEAR references Government of Canada instruments because they provide a
> recognizable federal operating baseline. In provincial, territorial, municipal, Crown, or broader
> public-sector contexts, the same method should be mapped to the applicable local policy, privacy,
> accessibility, language, records, digital, and audit requirements.

| Obligation / instrument | Why it matters | How CLEAR supports it | What CLEAR does not replace |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Policy on Service and Digital** | Sets integrated rules for managing service delivery, information and data, IT, and cyber in the digital era. | Helps organize evidence of service continuity, information/records integrity, and decision context across change. | The department's own service, information, IT, and cyber management responsibilities and reporting. |
| **Directive on Service and Digital** | Operationalizes the policy for day-to-day management of services, information, data, and technology. | Helps surface where service logic, ownership, and evidence are thin before a change proceeds. | Departmental design, delivery, and management decisions. |
| **Directive on Automated Decision-Making** | Requires risk reduction, transparency, accountability, fairness, recourse, and impact assessment for systems that make or support decisions affecting clients. | Flags when a change involves automated support for client-affecting decisions and may require an Algorithmic Impact Assessment and related review. | The institution's automated-decision review, Algorithmic Impact Assessment, or approval processes. |
| **Directive on Privacy Practices** | Requires consistent privacy management, PIAs where applicable, mitigation measures, and periodic review. | Helps identify whether personal information is involved and whether a privacy office / PIA review may be needed; encourages minimization. | The Privacy Office, PIA process, or any privacy determination. |
| **Values and Ethics Code for the Public Sector** | Anchors expected behaviours and public confidence in institutions. | Helps organize evidence that a practice remains traceable to authority, fairness, and review expectations for human consideration. | The institution's ethics, conduct, and public-confidence responsibilities. |
| **GBA Plus** | Supports responsive and inclusive policies and programs by identifying who is impacted and anticipating barriers. | Helps surface whether GBA Plus considerations remain visible and traceable in the operating layer. | The institution's GBA Plus analysis and program design decisions. |
| **Accessibility / Accessible Canada Act** | Aims for a barrier-free Canada by identifying, removing, and preventing barriers in federal jurisdiction. | Helps track whether accessibility commitments and barrier-mitigation evidence survive modernization. | Accessibility assessments, remediation, or conformance determinations. |
| **Official Languages** | Addresses communications, services, bilingual capacity, and equal participation. | Prompts whether official-language obligations and evidence are preserved when a client-facing process or workflow changes. | The institution's official-language obligations and compliance. |
| **Access to Information / records and evidence management** | Gives people rights to access records and personal information held by institutions. | Encourages disciplined decision logs and an evidence source register while avoiding uncontrolled side records. | ATIP processing, records authorities, or disclosure decisions. |

CLEAR does not overclaim compliance. A CLEAR output may indicate the need for a departmental
review; it never substitutes for one.

## 12. Risk-tiering model

Not every institutional question carries the same sensitivity. CLEAR classifies each question into
one of four risk levels, and the evidence, privacy, legal, accessibility, official-language, and
automated-decision review expectations rise with the level.

| Level | Typical question | Evidence expectation | Required human-review posture | Escalation triggers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Level 1 — Low institutional sensitivity** | Internal process clarity with no client or rights impact. | Basic documentation; single-source acceptable with caveats. | Owner review sufficient. | Any client-facing or personal-information element appears. |
| **Level 2 — Internal operational sensitivity** | Continuity or ownership of an internal service or process. | Corroborated evidence for key claims; durability noted. | Service owner plus one accountable function. | Public visibility, workforce-planning, or evidence-fragmentation concerns. |
| **Level 3 — Public/client impact** | Continuity or accountability of a client-facing service. | Durable, corroborated, recent evidence; gaps named explicitly. | Program/service owner plus relevant functions (e.g., privacy, Official Languages, accessibility). | Rights, eligibility, equity, or automated-decision elements appear. |
| **Level 4 — Rights, eligibility, equity, privacy, legal, or high-trust impact** | Change affecting rights, benefits, access, recourse, or high public trust. | Strongest available evidence; explicit confidence, provenance, and gaps; conservative interpretation. | Executive sponsor plus mandatory involvement of privacy/ATIP, legal, equity/GBA Plus/accessibility/Official Languages, and automated-decision review as applicable. | Any automated decision support; any unresolved privacy, legal, or equity question. |

Higher-risk questions require more evidence, more accountable review, and more caution. When in
doubt, CLEAR tiers upward.

### Minimum evidence package by tier

To keep the tiers audit-grade rather than merely qualitative, each level defines a minimum evidence
package. Higher tiers include everything from the tiers below them.

- **Level 1:**
  - institutional question
  - accountable owner
  - evidence source
  - confidence caveat
- **Level 2:**
  - institutional question
  - scope and exclusions
  - service / process owner
  - evidence source register
  - confidence level
  - uncertainty and next evidence needed
- **Level 3:**
  - authority / policy basis
  - service owner
  - evidence source register
  - privacy / ATIP screen
  - accessibility / official-language / GBA Plus screen where relevant
  - confidence level by finding
  - accountable-function review
  - uncertainty and next evidence needed
- **Level 4:**
  - all Level 3 elements
  - executive sponsor
  - relevant accountable-function involvement before the final brief is treated as decision-grade
  - automated-decision trigger screen where applicable
  - privacy / legal / policy / accessibility / official-language / equity review flags as applicable
  - conservative interpretation of findings

## 13. Evidence confidence rubric

The four confidence levels in Section 5 remain the executive-facing summary. For audit
defensibility, each confidence judgment is now grounded in a dimensional rubric so that
"moderate confidence" means something consistent rather than subjective.

Confidence is assessed across seven dimensions:

| Dimension | Question |
| --- | --- |
| **Provenance** | Where did the evidence come from, and is the source authoritative? |
| **Durability** | Will it survive staff and system change? |
| **Corroboration** | Is it supported by more than one independent source? |
| **Recency** | Is it current enough for the decision at hand? |
| **Completeness** | Does it cover the question, or only part of it? |
| **Decision relevance** | Does it actually answer the institutional question? |
| **Sensitivity** | Does it involve personal or otherwise sensitive information that must be handled with care? |

These dimensions inform the summary confidence levels:

- **High confidence** — Strong across provenance, durability, corroboration, recency, completeness,
  and relevance; sensitivity handled appropriately.
- **Moderate confidence** — Solid on most dimensions but partial on one or more (e.g., single-source
  or dated); usable with stated caveats.
- **Low confidence** — Weak on several dimensions; points to attention, not a conclusion.
- **Insufficient evidence** — Too weak on relevance or completeness to support a judgment; the honest
  output is to name the gap.

Confidence describes **evidence quality**, not whether an institution is "good" or "bad." A low or
insufficient rating is a finding about the evidence base, and a signal about where to invest next.

### Evidence source quality

Confidence also depends on the *kind* of source. Some evidence is inherently stronger than other
evidence, and the rubric weights accordingly.

- **Higher-weight evidence:**
  - approved policy or authority
  - official decision record
  - system-of-record data
  - audit, evaluation, or control evidence
  - approved service standard or process document
  - formal complaints / recourse records
- **Lower-weight evidence:**
  - informal recollection
  - undocumented practice
  - single-person explanation
  - email fragments
  - stale spreadsheets
  - unapproved working notes

Lower-weight evidence can still be useful — it often points to where stronger evidence should be
sought — but it should not carry a high-confidence finding on its own.

## 14. Privacy, ATIP, and records posture

CLEAR is an evidence discipline, so it must be disciplined about the evidence it touches and the
records it creates. Its default posture is minimization and control.

- **Use existing institutional evidence where possible** rather than collecting anew.
- **Identify whether personal information is involved** at the start of any question.
- **Avoid unnecessary personal-data collection**; do not gather what the question does not require.
- **Aggregate where possible** and avoid sensitive individual-level profiling.
- **Flag when a Privacy Office review or PIA may be needed** — CLEAR raises the flag; it does not
  make the privacy determination.
- **Distinguish working notes from final human-reviewed outputs**, so drafts are not mistaken for
  conclusions.
- **Maintain an evidence source register** so every claim can be traced to where it came from.
- **Avoid uncontrolled inbox or spreadsheet evidence stores** and other side-channel records.
- **Recognize that records may be subject to institutional access and privacy obligations**, and
  keep decision logs disciplined accordingly.

The goal is to preserve genuine decision evidence while never creating sloppy, uncontrolled, or
excessive records.

## 15. Automation and AI trigger rule

CLEAR is not an AI method, and most CLEAR questions involve no automation at all. But when
automation appears, CLEAR applies an explicit trigger:

> **Trigger:** If a service change involves automated support for decisions affecting rights,
> interests, benefits, access, eligibility, recourse, or service outcomes, CLEAR must flag the need
> for the institution's applicable automated-decision, privacy, legal, policy, and program-authority
> review processes — including an Algorithmic Impact Assessment where required.

To be unambiguous:

> CLEAR does not replace the institution's automated-decision, privacy, legal, security,
> accessibility, official-language, or policy review processes. It helps surface when those
> processes should be engaged, and helps organize the evidence they will rely on.

## 16. Inclusion, accessibility, Official Languages, and GBA Plus traceability

Equity implementation traceability is deliberately widened here into the language Canadian public
institutions actually use. CLEAR treats all of the following as matters of **visibility and
traceability** for human review — never as automated findings.

- **GBA Plus traceability** — whether GBA Plus considerations remain visible from commitment through
  operation to review.
- **Accessibility** — whether accessibility commitments are visible in workflow design, whether
  barriers are identified and tracked, whether accessible service channels are considered, and
  whether accessibility evidence is retained through modernization. For digital or IT-enabled
  changes, accessibility traceability should include service channels, forms, web content,
  documents, workflows, assistive-technology compatibility, and evidence that barriers were
  identified and mitigated.
- **Official languages** — whether a service, communication, staffing model, or client-facing process
  affected by a change preserves official-language obligations and evidence. Official-language
  traceability may involve public communications, service channels, bilingual capacity, internal
  working-language obligations, staffing models, and evidence that obligations remain visible through
  workflow or system change.
- **Equity and anti-racism** — visible and traceable only. CLEAR checks whether stated commitments
  remain visible in the operating layer.
- **Indigenous / reconciliation considerations** — where a question may affect Indigenous peoples,
  rights, programs, services, data, or relationships, CLEAR should flag the need for the
  institution's applicable Indigenous relations, consultation, legal, policy, and program-authority
  processes. CLEAR does not make these determinations.

CLEAR produces **no automated findings of racism, discrimination, wrongdoing, or misconduct**, and
does not score, rank, or rate people or groups. Human review remains authoritative, and equity,
accessibility, and official-language functions retain their own authority over their domains.

## 17. Accountable-function role map

CLEAR organizes evidence for the accountable humans; it does not decide for any of them. The role
map below clarifies who owns what, when CLEAR should involve them, and what CLEAR never decides on
their behalf.

| Accountable function | What they own | When CLEAR should involve them | What CLEAR does not decide for them |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Program owner** | The program's mandate, outcomes, and authority. | When continuity, evidence, or accountability of the program is in question. | Whether the program is meeting its mandate. |
| **Service owner** | Design and delivery of the service. | When service logic, ownership, or continuity is under pressure. | How the service should be delivered. |
| **CIO / digital lead** | Information, data, IT, and cyber management. | When a modernization or automation change is in scope. | Technology and digital management decisions. |
| **Privacy / ATIP** | Privacy practices, PIAs, and access obligations. | When personal information or records/access questions appear. | Any privacy or disclosure determination. |
| **Legal** | Legal risk and interpretation. | When authority, rights, or legal exposure is implicated. | Legal advice or legal conclusions. |
| **Internal audit / evaluation** | Independent assurance and evaluation. | When evidence or accountability gaps are surfaced. | Audit findings or assurance opinions. |
| **Equity / GBA Plus / accessibility / Official Languages** | Their respective inclusion and obligation domains. | When a change may affect equity, accessibility, or official-language obligations. | Any equity, accessibility, or official-language determination. |
| **HR / workforce planning** | Workforce, staffing, and knowledge transfer. | When workforce transition or knowledge concentration is in question. | HR, staffing, or employment decisions. |
| **Executive sponsor** | Overall accountability for the decision. | For Level 3–4 questions and any high-trust change. | The decision itself, which remains theirs. |

## 18. Standard output templates

Templates turn CLEAR from a concept into a repeatable method. Each CLEAR output follows a standard
skeleton so that a reader — including internal audit — always knows what is inside.

**Shared skeleton (all outputs):**

1. Institutional question
2. Scope and exclusions
3. Evidence sources reviewed (with evidence source register reference)
4. CLEAR pillar findings (Continuity, Legitimacy, Evidence, Accountability, Readiness)
5. Confidence level by finding (with rubric dimensions noted)
6. Uncertainty and evidence gaps
7. Accountable owner(s) / functions
8. Recommended next evidence
9. Human-review boundary statement

The five named outputs adapt that skeleton:

- **Public-service continuity brief** — executive-readable; emphasizes Continuity and Accountability
  findings, risk tier, and risks requiring attention.
- **Evidence confidence note** — focuses on Section 4 (findings) and Section 5 (confidence by
  dimension) for a specific commitment or claim.
- **Implementation traceability map** — traces a commitment from policy/authority through operation to
  review, marking where the thread breaks.
- **Modernization readiness summary** — centers Readiness findings and the automation/AI trigger
  outcome, stating whether a change is safe and reviewable before it becomes the default.
- **Human-review risk register** — a running list of surfaced signals, each with risk tier, confidence
  level, accountable owner, and next evidence need.

Every template ends with the human-review boundary and defers the decision to the accountable
authority.

## 19. The department owns the conclusion

CLEAR outputs are inputs to human judgment, not verdicts.

> CLEAR outputs do not bind the institution. The department, agency, Crown corporation, public body,
> or accountable authority remains responsible for interpreting evidence, applying law and policy,
> consulting required functions, and making decisions through its own authorities.

This is what keeps CLEAR firmly on the right side of the line: it is an evidence discipline that
strengthens accountable human decision-making, not a shadow governance layer that displaces it.
