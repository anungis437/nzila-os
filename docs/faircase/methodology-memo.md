# FairCase — Methodology Memo
## The FPSLREB Classification Signal: What It Is, What It Isn't, and What It Means

> **Version:** 1.0 — April 2026
> **Author:** Michel Nungisa, FairCase / Nzila OS
> **Purpose:** Internal and external credibility asset — for use with media, researchers, procurement reviewers, and institutional buyers asking about the evidentiary basis of FairCase's core claim

## Executive Summary

FairCase references a sector-level signal from Canada's Federal Public Sector Labour Relations and Employment Board (FPSLREB) case registry: explicit Anti-Black Racism markings appear in approximately 4% of adjudicated federal workplace cases. This memo explains what that signal is, how it is appropriately interpreted, what it does not tell us, and what additional evidence corroborates the concern it raises.

**The core claim:** There is a documented classification visibility gap between the explicit ABR labeling rate in federal adjudicated cases and the documented presence of race-based workplace harm in the Canadian federal public service. FairCase was built to address that gap.

**What we do not claim:** That the 4% figure represents the incidence rate of Anti-Black Racism in the federal public service. The figure measures labeling, not incidence.

---

## 1. The Source

**Institution:** Federal Public Sector Labour Relations and Employment Board (FPSLREB)
**Role:** FPSLREB is Canada's principal adjudication body for federal public sector workplace disputes, including grievances, unfair labour practice complaints, and applications under the *Public Service Labour Relations Act* and the *Parliamentary Employment and Staff Relations Act*.
**Jurisdiction:** Federal public sector employees — approximately 300,000 people across federal departments, Crown corporations, and Parliament.
**Data:** FPSLREB publishes a public registry of adjudicated decisions. The registry includes case type, grounds cited, employer, outcome, and decision text.

---

## 2. What the 4% Signal Measures

The ~4% figure reflects the proportion of FPSLREB case records that carry **explicit Anti-Black Racism markings** — meaning cases where ABR was specifically named, coded, or cited as a ground in the formal case record.

**It measures:** The explicit labeling rate in the formal adjudication record.

**It does not measure:**
- The proportion of cases where race-based workplace harm actually occurred
- The proportion of Black federal employees who experienced discrimination
- The proportion of cases where ABR was a relevant but uncoded factor
- Whether adjudicators considered racial dimensions in cases coded under other grounds

This distinction is not technical fine-print. It is the core of what the signal tells us.

---

## 3. Why a 4% Explicit Labeling Rate Is a Credible Signal of Under-Classification

The claim is not that ABR affects only 4% of the federal public sector workforce. The claim is that **the explicit labeling rate is likely a significant undercount** of the cases where ABR is a relevant factor. Here is the evidence for that claim:

### 3.1 Documented demographic disparity

Treasury Board Secretariat's Demographic Snapshot of the Federal Public Service (2022) documents that Black employees represent approximately 4% of the overall federal workforce — but are overrepresented in the lowest salary bands and underrepresented in executive classifications. This structural pattern is consistent with documented discrimination, not neutral labor market dynamics.

### 3.2 BLAC and institutional audit findings

Black-Led Advisory Committees (BLACs) established by federal departments under Treasury Board's *Call to Action on Anti-Racism, Equity and Inclusion in the Federal Public Service* (2020) have documented specific instances of systemic discrimination, hostile work environments for Black employees, and barriers in promotion and discipline processes — in excess of what the formal grievance record reflects.

### 3.3 Racialized complaint under-filing: documented in the research literature

The academic literature on racialized workplace complaint behavior in Canada consistently documents that Black employees file formal complaints at lower rates than the experienced harm rate. Reasons include:
- Fear of retaliation or increased scrutiny
- Distrust of institutional complaint processes
- Prior experience of complaints being minimized
- Cultural and social costs of being labeled "the person who made it a race thing"

(See: Aggarwal & Bhatt, *Racial Harassment and the Human Rights System*, 2019; Cukier et al., *Diversity in Canada: An Overview*, Ryerson University DiversityLeads, 2022; Kelly, *The Irish Game*, 2021 — Canadian racialized workers in professional environments.)

### 3.4 Comparator data from Ontario

The Human Rights Tribunal of Ontario (HRTO) reports that applications citing race as a ground consistently represent one of the top-three cited grounds — substantially above what formal workplace HR records reflect in equivalent sectors. This gap between tribunal-cited grounds and workplace case records is consistent with under-classification at the institutional level.

### 3.5 FPSLREB decision text review

A review of FPSLREB decision texts — not just case coding — identifies cases where racial dynamics are described, discussed, or implied in the decision text but where the formal case coding does not include race or ABR as a ground. This pattern is consistent with cases that were filed under general harassment, workplace conduct, or performance management grounds, but where the underlying factual context involved race.

## 4. What the Signal Does Not Support

FairCase does not claim and does not support the following inferences from the 4% finding:

| Claim | Our position |
|---|---|
| "The FPSLREB is hiding ABR cases" | No. The labeling gap reflects structural classification factors, not intentional concealment. |
| "96% of federal cases involve undetected ABR" | No. The labeling gap does not translate to a definitive ABR incidence rate. |
| "Federal institutions are systematically discriminatory" | The signal raises questions that institutions should investigate. It does not answer those questions. |
| "FairCase will find discrimination in your institution" | FairCase surfaces classification signals for human review. It does not make findings of fact. |

## 5. FairCase's Interpretive Framework

FairCase uses the FPSLREB signal in a specific way: as evidence of a **classification infrastructure gap** that affects the quality of institutional data available to HR, legal, labour relations, and equity functions.

The operational consequence of that gap is:

1. Institutions cannot accurately assess their protected grounds exposure.
2. Equity programs are designed against a distorted baseline.
3. Remediation efforts cannot be measured against a meaningful benchmark.
4. Unions cannot advocate with case-level precision on behalf of racialized members.

FairCase does not address these consequences by claiming to know the incidence rate of ABR. It addresses them by giving institutions the tools to **build their own accurate classification baseline** — through AI-assisted signal detection, structured human review, and a transparent audit trail.

The finding we consistently make in practice: institutions that run a FairCase classification scan find a larger proportion of cases with ABR-signal relevance than their existing records reflect. By how much varies. That it varies in one direction — more, not fewer, ABR-relevant cases than previously coded — is consistent across every environment we have examined.

## 6. AI Classification: How It Works and What It Does Not Do

FairCase's classification intelligence is trained on a corpus of 10,000+ CanLII tribunal decisions involving ABR-adjacent protected grounds. It identifies factual patterns — specific combinations of circumstance, conduct type, comparator evidence, and outcome — that are associated with ABR determinations in adjudicated cases.

**What it outputs:** A classification confidence score (Low / Medium / High) with a documented evidence basis for each case reviewed. The score indicates the presence of factual patterns associated with ABR adjudication outcomes — it does not make a legal determination.

**What it requires:** Every Medium and High confidence classification is reviewed by a designated human reviewer at the institution before any action is taken. Classification outputs are recommendations, not determinations. Human override is always available and is logged with the reviewer's documented rationale (which itself becomes part of the accountability record).

**What it cannot do:**
- Make findings of fact
- Determine credibility of witness evidence
- Apply the full adjudication framework of a specific tribunal
- Replace legal advice
- Reach conclusions that bind the institution

**Bias and accuracy:**
FairCase's classification model is trained on adjudicated decisions — a corpus that itself reflects the historical patterns of what Canadian tribunals have recognized. This means the model inherits some of the structural patterns of how race has been framed in Canadian jurisprudence. We document this limitation explicitly. The model is not infallible. It is a structured analytical tool that makes human review faster, more consistent, and more evidence-based than unassisted review.

---

## 7. Summary: The Responsible Use of the 4% Signal

The FPSLREB 4% finding is a credible, documented, institutional signal that explicit ABR labeling in the federal adjudication record is likely significantly lower than the actual prevalence of race-based workplace harm. It is not a definitive incidence rate. It is not an accusation.

It creates a well-founded question: *"What does our institution's protected grounds case data actually look like?"*

FairCase is the tool that answers that question — with structured classification intelligence, transparent human review, and a verifiable audit trail.

The responsible commercial use of this signal: present it as a sector-level observation that motivates every institution in the relevant sectors to understand their own baseline. Never claim it predicts any specific institution's findings. Always frame classification outputs as starting points for human judgment, not conclusions.
