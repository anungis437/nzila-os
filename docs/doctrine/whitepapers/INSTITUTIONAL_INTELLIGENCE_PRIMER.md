## Preface

This primer is not a philosophy document.

It is a practical framework for institutional leaders who recognize the problem described in *Introducing Institutional Intelligence* and want to understand what building Institutional Intelligence capacity actually looks like in practice.

It answers four questions:

1. How do I know how much Institutional Intelligence my organization has?
2. What does the path to improvement look like?
3. What does implementation actually require?
4. How is this different across the sectors I operate in?

The primer assumes familiarity with the Institutional Intelligence category definition. If you are reading this without having read *Introducing Institutional Intelligence* or *What Is Institutional Intelligence?*, start there.

---

## Part 1. The Institutional Intelligence Maturity Model

### Why Maturity Models Work

Maturity models succeed when they describe real organizational states rather than aspirational ideals. The five levels below reflect patterns observed across labour organizations, public sector bodies, healthcare institutions, and associations — not theoretical categories.

The most important property of this model: most organizations are not at one level across the board. They are typically at different levels across different dimensions. That asymmetry is precisely where intervention is most valuable.

---

### The Five Levels

#### Level 1 — Personality Dependent

The institution runs on people, not systems.

Critical knowledge lives in the heads of two or three long-serving individuals. Decisions are made informally and not documented. Governance exists on paper but is not consistently practiced. When key individuals leave, the organization does not just lose a person — it loses years of institutional context that cannot be reconstructed.

**How it presents:**
- New staff describe their onboarding as "figuring it out as I go"
- Past decisions cannot be explained without calling the person who made them
- Leadership transitions trigger 6–12 month operational disruption
- Governance outcomes vary significantly depending on who is handling a file

**Risk profile:** High continuity risk; moderate-to-high governance risk; low explainability under scrutiny.

---

#### Level 2 — Fragmented Documentation

The institution has begun to capture knowledge, but not systematically.

Some processes are documented. Some decisions have rationale recorded. But the documents live in disconnected places — a shared drive here, an email archive there, a personal folder that one coordinator maintains. The knowledge exists but cannot be reliably retrieved, and its accuracy degrades as circumstances change.

**How it presents:**
- Staff know documentation exists but cannot consistently find it
- Different teams operate with different versions of the same policy
- Governance decisions produce inconsistent outcomes across teams
- External audits require significant preparation

**Risk profile:** Moderate continuity risk; elevated governance fragmentation risk.

---

#### Level 3 — Structured Operations

The institution has standardized its core processes and created consistent governance practices.

Knowledge is captured in recognized systems. Policies are maintained and accessible. Decision workflows are documented. But the institution has not yet integrated these elements into a coherent institutional intelligence layer. Memory, governance, and continuity operate as separate functions rather than as a unified system.

**How it presents:**
- Core operations survive normal staff transitions
- Governance decisions are mostly consistent, with documented exceptions
- Some institutional history is retrievable but rationale is often missing
- Leadership transitions cause moderate disruption but not crisis

**Risk profile:** Moderate continuity risk; manageable governance risk; gaps in decision accountability.

---

#### Level 4 — Governed Continuity

The institution operates with explicit accountability architecture, preserved decision lineage, and deliberate continuity design.

Knowledge is institutionalized rather than individualized. Governance decisions include rationale that is preserved and retrievable. Transitions are managed with explicit handover protocols. The institution can explain its past decisions and demonstrate that its authority is exercised consistently.

**How it presents:**
- New leaders are operationally capable within weeks, not months
- Governance decisions can be audited and explained without preparation
- The institution can demonstrate accountability to members, regulators, or the public
- Policy evolution has a traceable lineage

**Risk profile:** Low continuity risk; low governance risk; strong legitimacy posture.

---

#### Level 5 — Institutional Intelligence

The institution operates as a learning system. Memory, continuity, governance, and trust are integrated and mutually reinforcing. The institution does not just preserve knowledge — it converts experience into institutional learning that improves future decisions.

**How it presents:**
- Post-incident reviews produce documented governance updates
- The institution can demonstrate improving legitimacy indicators over time
- Trust is measured as an operational metric, not assumed
- Resilience under disruption is demonstrably stronger than peer institutions
- Governance design is proactive, not reactive

**Risk profile:** Minimal continuity and governance risk; strong and measurable resilience.

---

### Where Most Institutions Actually Are

Based on discovery across labour, healthcare, municipal, and association contexts:

- Approximately 40–50% of institutions operate primarily at Level 1 or Level 2.
- Approximately 35–40% are at Level 3.
- Fewer than 15% have reached Level 4.
- Level 5 is rare and typically partial — strong in some dimensions, weaker in others.

The gap between Level 2 and Level 3 is the most common transition point. It is also where most structured improvement programs stall — not because the organization lacks intent, but because it lacks the governance architecture and stewardship discipline to sustain the gains.

## Part 2. The Institutional Intelligence Assessment (IIA)

### Purpose

The IIA provides a structured diagnostic of an institution's current Institutional Intelligence capacity across six dimensions.

It is designed to be administered as:
- A leadership discovery workshop (3–4 hours)
- A governance diagnostic (self-administered, 30–45 minutes)
- An annual institutional review (integrated into existing governance cycle)

### The Six Dimensions

#### Dimension 1 — Memory Integrity

**What it measures:** How reliably the institution preserves contextual knowledge — not just records, but rationale, accountability lineage, and interpretable history.

**Indicators:**
1. Decision rationale capture rate: percentage of significant decisions with documented reasoning and named accountable authority.
2. Retrievability score: ability to locate and interpret a decision from three or more years ago without contacting the original decision-maker.
3. Knowledge concentration risk: number of critical knowledge dependencies held by three or fewer individuals.
4. Onboarding velocity: average time for a new leader to reach operational competency without extended mentorship.

**Assessment questions:**
- If the three people who have been here longest left tomorrow, what would the organization be unable to do or explain?
- Can you retrieve the rationale for a major policy decision made five years ago? Two years ago?
- How many critical processes exist only in one person's head?

---

#### Dimension 2 — Continuity Capacity

**What it measures:** The institution's ability to remain operationally coherent through transitions, disruptions, and change.

**Indicators:**
1. Transition recovery time: documented or estimated time to full operational capacity after a senior leadership change.
2. Process redundancy: percentage of critical processes with a documented backup owner.
3. Handover completeness: existence and quality of transition protocols for key roles.
4. Dependency mapping: documented map of people-critical dependencies.

**Assessment questions:**
- What would happen operationally if your most experienced team member left in two weeks?
- How long did your last leadership transition take to absorb?
- Which roles have no documented continuity plan?

---

#### Dimension 3 — Governance Maturity

**What it measures:** The degree to which governance decisions are consistent, attributable, explainable, and defensible.

**Indicators:**
1. Decision traceability: percentage of significant governance decisions with traceable records.
2. Governance consistency score: variance in outcomes for equivalent governance decisions across teams or time periods.
3. Shadow governance presence: number of identified informal practices that override or circumvent formal governance.
4. Policy currency: percentage of active policies reviewed within the required cycle.

**Assessment questions:**
- Can you explain a governance decision made under the previous leadership team without contacting them?
- Do equivalent governance situations reliably produce equivalent outcomes?
- Where does your informal governance practice diverge from your documented governance?

---

#### Dimension 4 — Trust Operations

**What it measures:** The institution's operational trust posture — the degree to which its behavior reliably produces trust as a measurable outcome.

**Indicators:**
1. Explainability response time: average time to produce a clear explanation of a governance decision when challenged.
2. Challenge and appeal completion rate: percentage of formal challenges resolved with documented outcomes.
3. Dispute recurrence rate: frequency of equivalent disputes occurring more than once (indicating unresolved systemic issues).
4. Transparency consistency: degree to which communication of governance decisions is complete, timely, and accurate.

**Assessment questions:**
- How long does it typically take to produce a clear, documented response to a governance challenge?
- How often do the same types of disputes recur?
- When did you last proactively communicate a governance decision and its rationale without being asked?

---

#### Dimension 5 — Accountability Architecture

**What it measures:** The clarity and enforceability of accountability across the institution's governance and operational decisions.

**Indicators:**
1. Decision rights coverage: percentage of significant decision types with explicitly mapped authority.
2. Accountability chain completeness: ability to trace any significant outcome to a named responsible steward.
3. Escalation reliability: existence and use of defined escalation paths for contested or high-risk decisions.
4. Human oversight coverage: percentage of consequential automated or AI-assisted decisions with defined human review requirements.

**Assessment questions:**
- For any significant governance decision made in the last year, can you name the accountable authority without uncertainty?
- What happens when a decision falls between defined accountability zones?
- Where does your escalation process break down under real conditions?

---

#### Dimension 6 — Institutional Resilience

**What it measures:** The institution's demonstrated capacity to absorb disruption while preserving governance integrity and legitimacy.

**Indicators:**
1. Recovery coherence: degree to which the institution maintains governance consistency during and after disruption.
2. Post-incident learning rate: percentage of significant incidents that produce documented governance updates.
3. Legitimacy stability: degree to which stakeholder trust remains stable during transition and disruption periods.
4. Sovereignty posture: degree of operational independence from single vendors, platforms, or individuals.

**Assessment questions:**
- What was the last significant institutional disruption? How did governance hold up?
- How many significant incidents in the last two years produced changes to documented governance?
- If your primary platform vendor disappeared tomorrow, what would you lose access to?

---

### IIA Scoring Guide

Score each dimension 0–4 using the rubric below, then compute the composite IIA score.

| Score | Level | Descriptor |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Absent | No systematic approach exists |
| 1 | Ad hoc | Individual efforts without institutional consistency |
| 2 | Developing | Systematic approach in progress, inconsistently applied |
| 3 | Established | Consistent practice with documented evidence |
| 4 | Optimizing | Continuous improvement with measurable outcomes |

**Composite IIA Score:** Sum of all six dimension scores (maximum 24).

| Composite | Maturity Level | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| 0–6 | Level 1 Personality Dependent | Immediate continuity risk intervention |
| 7–12 | Level 2 Fragmented Documentation | Structured documentation and governance design |
| 13–17 | Level 3 Structured Operations | Integration and accountability architecture |
| 18–21 | Level 4 Governed Continuity | Learning system design and trust operations |
| 22–24 | Level 5 Institutional Intelligence | Continuous calibration and peer leadership |

## Part 3. Adoption Roadmap

### The Four Phases of Institutional Intelligence Development

Most institutions do not move from Level 1 to Level 5 in a single initiative. They move through identifiable phases, each with its own primary focus, typical duration, and critical success factors.

---

#### Phase 1 — Foundation (Levels 1 to 2)

**Primary focus:** Stabilize critical knowledge and establish baseline governance documentation.

**Key activities:**
1. Knowledge risk audit: identify the five to ten highest-risk knowledge concentrations.
2. Critical process documentation: capture the processes that cannot fail.
3. Basic governance documentation: document the decisions that currently operate informally.
4. Transition readiness baseline: assess what would break immediately in a leadership transition.

**Critical success factor:** Executive sponsor who treats this as institutional risk management, not administrative overhead.

**Typical duration:** 3–6 months.

**Do not move forward until:** Critical knowledge concentrations are mapped and primary risk is documented.

---

#### Phase 2 — Structure (Levels 2 to 3)

**Primary focus:** Build consistent governance practices and integrate institutional knowledge into accessible systems.

**Key activities:**
1. Governance architecture: define decision rights, accountability chains, and escalation paths.
2. Policy governance: establish review cycles, ownership, and version control.
3. Decision documentation standard: define what records a significant governance decision must produce.
4. Onboarding design: create transition protocols for key roles.

**Critical success factor:** Governance design that is operationally embedded, not administratively imposed.

**Typical duration:** 6–12 months.

**Do not move forward until:** Governance decisions are traceable and policy ownership is assigned.

---

#### Phase 3 — Integration (Levels 3 to 4)

**Primary focus:** Integrate memory, governance, and continuity into a coherent accountability architecture.

**Key activities:**
1. Decision lineage system: preserve rationale alongside records.
2. Trust operations: establish explainability standards and challenge response SLAs.
3. Accountability mapping: map every significant decision type to named authority.
4. Continuity protocols: formalize transition handover packages for all key roles.
5. IIA baseline assessment: establish formal measurement.

**Critical success factor:** Leadership commitment to accountability architecture as permanent infrastructure, not a project.

**Typical duration:** 12–18 months.

**Do not move forward until:** IIA score is consistently above 17 and governance can be audited without preparation.

---

#### Phase 4 — Optimization (Level 5)

**Primary focus:** Convert experience into institutional learning. Measure trust as an operational outcome. Build proactive resilience.

**Key activities:**
1. Post-incident governance reviews: mandatory documentation updates from significant events.
2. Trust measurement: operationalize trust indicators as a periodic governance metric.
3. Institutional learning loops: connect governance outcomes to policy refinement cycles.
4. Peer leadership: contribute to sector-level Institutional Intelligence standards.

**Critical success factor:** Culture of institutional stewardship at every leadership level, not just the governance team.

**Typical duration:** Ongoing.

## Part 4. Governance Operating Model

### The Three Stewardship Roles

Institutional Intelligence requires three types of stewardship accountability, which may be held by individuals or teams depending on organizational scale.

**Operational Stewards** own day-to-day knowledge integrity and governance consistency. They ensure that decisions produce records, that processes are followed as documented, and that exceptions are flagged rather than absorbed silently.

**Domain Stewards** own policy coherence within their area. They maintain policy currency, resolve interpretation conflicts, and ensure that governance decisions in their domain are consistent with institutional commitments.

**Constitutional Stewards** own doctrine and framework conformance. They monitor for governance drift, lead IIA assessments, manage doctrine amendments, and ensure that new initiatives are evaluated against Institutional Intelligence principles before launch.

---

### The Governance Cycle

Institutional Intelligence requires a recurring governance cycle, not a one-time implementation.

| Cycle | Frequency | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Decision review | Ongoing | Ensure significant decisions produce records |
| Policy review | Annual | Currency and ownership confirmation |
| IIA assessment | Annual | Maturity measurement and priority setting |
| Continuity drill | Annual | Transition readiness validation |
| Doctrine review | Annual | Framework conformance and lessons integration |
| Constitutional review | Every 3–5 years | Framework revision and strategic realignment |

## Part 5. Sector-Specific Patterns

### Labour Organizations

**Primary Institutional Intelligence challenge:** Preserving negotiation memory, grievance precedent, and governance consistency across officer cycles and staff transitions.

**Most common failure pattern:** Negotiating team changes every few years; new team does not have access to the history of what was offered, refused, traded, and why.

**Highest-impact early investments:**
1. Negotiation memory system: structured capture of positions, rationale, and outcomes.
2. Grievance precedent registry: searchable, accountable record of interpretations and decisions.
3. Officer transition protocol: formal handover package including open files, commitments, and relationship context.

**IIA priority dimensions:** Memory Integrity and Governance Maturity.

---

### Healthcare Institutions

**Primary Institutional Intelligence challenge:** Maintaining clinical and governance accountability across multidisciplinary teams, shift patterns, and significant staff turnover.

**Most common failure pattern:** Clinical protocol governance is disconnected from the rationale that produced the protocol; when challenged, the protocol cannot be explained.

**Highest-impact early investments:**
1. Clinical decision lineage: capture rationale alongside clinical protocols.
2. Accountability architecture: explicit mapping of who is accountable for what across the care pathway.
3. Governance explainability standard: define what records a clinical governance decision must produce.

**IIA priority dimensions:** Accountability Architecture and Trust Operations.

---

### Municipal and Public Institutions

**Primary Institutional Intelligence challenge:** Maintaining commitment continuity and policy coherence across electoral cycles and political transitions.

**Most common failure pattern:** New administration inherits commitments from previous administration that are not documented or not visible; discovers them under public pressure or legal challenge.

**Highest-impact early investments:**
1. Commitment registry: documented record of public and formal commitments with named accountability.
2. Transition package design: structured transfer of context for senior roles across electoral cycles.
3. Policy lineage system: traceable history of how policies evolved and why.

**IIA priority dimensions:** Continuity Capacity and Accountability Architecture.

---

### Associations and Non-Profits

**Primary Institutional Intelligence challenge:** Sustaining governance credibility and mission continuity through high volunteer leadership turnover and funding volatility.

**Most common failure pattern:** Long-serving executive director retires; successor discovers that significant governance decisions were made verbally and are not documented anywhere.

**Highest-impact early investments:**
1. Governance decision register: documented record of all significant governance decisions with rationale.
2. Role continuity protocols: transition package for all board officer and senior staff roles.
3. Relationship continuity: documented institutional relationship context for key external relationships.

**IIA priority dimensions:** Memory Integrity and Continuity Capacity.

## Part 7. Starting Points for Leadership Teams

The primer is a framework. The starting point for any specific organization depends on its current IIA profile and the most pressing institutional risks.

**If you are at Level 1 or 2:** The most valuable action you can take in the next 90 days is a knowledge risk audit. Identify the five to ten most critical institutional knowledge concentrations and assess the risk of losing each one. That audit alone will produce the business case for everything that follows.

**If you are at Level 3:** The highest-leverage investment is governance accountability architecture. Assign explicit decision rights, establish traceability standards, and define what a significant governance decision must produce in terms of records. The gap between Level 3 and Level 4 is almost always an accountability architecture gap.

**If you are at Level 4:** The transition to Level 5 is a culture transition as much as a systems transition. It requires deliberate learning loops — mechanisms that convert incidents, disputes, and near-misses into documented governance updates. The question to ask is: in the last twelve months, how many significant events produced documented governance improvements?

---

## Closing

Institutional Intelligence is not a destination. It is an operating condition — one that has to be actively maintained, measured, and improved over time.

The institutions that take it seriously will be the ones that remain legitimate, trustworthy, and resilient in an era when all three of those properties are increasingly difficult to sustain.

The work of building Institutional Intelligence capacity is the work of honoring institutional obligation — to members, to constituents, to patients, to workers, to the public.

That obligation does not belong to any single leadership team. It belongs to the institution across time.

Building the capacity to honor it is what this framework is for.
