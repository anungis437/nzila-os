> Doctrine without measurement is philosophy.
> The IIA converts Institutional Intelligence into a measurable, actionable diagnostic.

---

## Purpose

The Institutional Intelligence Assessment (IIA) provides a structured, evidence-based picture of an institution's current capacity across the six dimensions that determine long-term institutional resilience and legitimacy.

It is not an audit. It is not a scorecard for comparison against peers. It is a diagnostic tool that identifies where an institution is strong, where it is fragile, and where investment will have the highest leverage.

---

## How to Use This Assessment

The IIA can be administered in three modes:

**Discovery mode** — Leadership team completes the assessment independently (30–45 minutes). Produces a baseline profile for discussion.

**Workshop mode** — Facilitator-led session with senior leadership (3–4 hours). Produces a shared, validated profile and priority agreement.

**Annual review mode** — Integrated into existing governance cycle. Produces year-over-year trend data and a standing executive scorecard.

In all modes: score each dimension using direct evidence, not aspiration. Score what actually exists, not what was intended.

---

## Scoring Rubric (Apply to Every Dimension)

| Score | Level | What it actually means |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Absent | No systematic approach exists in practice |
| 1 | Ad hoc | Individual efforts without institutional consistency or reliability |
| 2 | Developing | Systematic approach exists but is inconsistently applied |
| 3 | Established | Consistent practice with documented evidence |
| 4 | Optimizing | Continuous improvement cycle with measurable outcomes |

Score on evidence, not intent. If a process exists on paper but is not practiced, score at 1 or 2, not 3.

## Dimension 1: Memory Integrity

**What is being measured:** How reliably the institution preserves contextual knowledge — not just records, but rationale, accountability lineage, and retrievable history.

### Indicators

| Indicator | Questions to Answer |
|---|---|
| Decision rationale capture | What % of significant decisions have documented reasoning and named accountable authority? |
| Retrievability | Can a decision from 3+ years ago be located and interpreted without contacting the original decision-maker? |
| Knowledge concentration risk | How many critical processes or decisions are held by 3 or fewer individuals? |
| Onboarding velocity | How long does it take a new leader or key staff member to reach full operational competency? |

### Assessment Questions

1. If your three most experienced people left this month, what would the organization be unable to do or explain?
2. Retrieve a significant governance decision from five years ago. Can you explain why it was made, who was accountable, and what was considered?
3. How many critical processes exist only in someone's head?
4. How long did your last major onboarding take before the new person was fully effective?

### Scoring Guide

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 0 | Critical knowledge exists only in individuals; no documentation of decisions or rationale |
| 1 | Some documents exist but are scattered; rationale rarely captured; retrieval depends on institutional memory |
| 2 | Documentation culture emerging; rationale captured for some decisions; significant gaps remain |
| 3 | Most significant decisions documented with rationale; knowledge retrievable without originator |
| 4 | Systematic decision lineage; knowledge concentration mapped and actively managed; onboarding measurably accelerated |

## Dimension 2: Continuity Capacity

**What is being measured:** The institution's ability to remain operationally coherent through leadership transitions, disruptions, and organizational change.

### Indicators

| Indicator | Questions to Answer |
|---|---|
| Transition recovery time | How long does full operational recovery take after a senior leadership change? |
| Process redundancy | What % of critical processes have a documented backup owner? |
| Handover completeness | Do formal transition protocols exist for key roles? Have they been used? |
| Dependency mapping | Is there a documented map of people-critical operational dependencies? |

### Assessment Questions

1. When was your last significant leadership transition? How long did operational disruption last?
2. For your three most critical operational roles: if the person in that role left in two weeks, who could take over and how prepared would they be?
3. Does a formal transition package exist for your CEO or Executive Director role? Has it been tested?
4. What would immediately break if your most tenured operational staff member left tomorrow?

### Scoring Guide

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 0 | No transition protocols; all continuity is person-dependent |
| 1 | Informal arrangements; some verbal knowledge transfer; no documented protocols |
| 2 | Partial documentation; some roles have transition materials; not consistently maintained |
| 3 | Formal transition protocols for most key roles; continuity dependencies mapped; handover tested |
| 4 | Proactive continuity design; regular continuity drills; measured recovery time; low person-dependency |

## Dimension 3: Governance Maturity

**What is being measured:** The degree to which governance decisions are consistent, attributable, explainable, and defensible over time.

### Indicators

| Indicator | Questions to Answer |
|---|---|
| Decision traceability | What % of significant governance decisions have traceable records? |
| Governance consistency | Do equivalent governance situations produce consistent outcomes across teams and over time? |
| Shadow governance presence | How many informal practices override or circumvent formal governance? |
| Policy currency | What % of active policies have been reviewed within the required cycle? |

### Assessment Questions

1. Pick a significant governance decision made under the previous leadership team. Can you explain why it was made without contacting anyone from that era?
2. If the same governance situation occurred in two different teams today, would it be handled the same way?
3. Where does your actual governance practice diverge from your documented governance?
4. When were your three most important policies last reviewed? Who owns them?

### Scoring Guide

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 0 | Governance is almost entirely informal; no traceability; high inconsistency |
| 1 | Some formal governance exists but is irregularly practiced; shadow governance dominant |
| 2 | Formal governance practiced inconsistently; policies exist but are not current; some traceability |
| 3 | Governance decisions consistently traceable; policies maintained; formal practice dominates |
| 4 | Governance excellence; complete traceability; low variance; policy lifecycle governed; shadow governance minimal |

## Dimension 4: Trust Operations

**What is being measured:** The institution's operational trust posture — the degree to which its behavior reliably and measurably produces trust as an outcome, not a claim.

### Indicators

| Indicator | Questions to Answer |
|---|---|
| Explainability response time | How long does it take to produce a clear documented response to a governance challenge? |
| Challenge resolution quality | What % of formal challenges are resolved with documented outcomes and clear rationale? |
| Dispute recurrence rate | How often do equivalent types of disputes recur (indicating unresolved systemic issues)? |
| Transparency consistency | Is governance communication complete, timely, and accurate without being prompted? |

### Assessment Questions

1. When was the last time a governance decision was formally challenged? How long did it take to produce a clear documented response?
2. How often do the same types of disputes recur?
3. In the last year, how many times did you proactively communicate a governance decision and its rationale without being asked?
4. Can you demonstrate improving trust indicators over time — not through survey sentiment, but through operational metrics?

### Scoring Guide

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 0 | Trust managed reactively through relationships; no measurement; no systematic transparency |
| 1 | Some transparency practices; challenges handled informally; no measurement of trust outcomes |
| 2 | Formal challenge processes exist; some documented outcomes; limited proactive transparency |
| 3 | Explainability standards established; challenges resolved with documentation; trust measured periodically |
| 4 | Trust operated as a standing metric; proactive transparency standard; dispute recurrence declining; demonstrable trend data |

## Dimension 5: Accountability Architecture

**What is being measured:** The clarity, coverage, and enforceability of accountability across governance and consequential operational decisions.

### Indicators

| Indicator | Questions to Answer |
|---|---|
| Decision rights coverage | What % of significant decision types have explicitly mapped authority? |
| Accountability chain completeness | Can any significant outcome be traced to a named accountable steward? |
| Escalation reliability | Are escalation paths defined, documented, and actually used? |
| Human oversight coverage | For consequential automated or AI-assisted decisions: are human review requirements defined? |

### Assessment Questions

1. For a major governance decision made last month: can you immediately name the accountable authority without uncertainty?
2. What happens when a significant decision falls between defined accountability zones?
3. When did your escalation process last work correctly under real pressure? When did it last fail?
4. If an AI-assisted recommendation led to a significant outcome, who is accountable? Is that written down?

### Scoring Guide

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 0 | Accountability is assumed, not designed; no explicit decision rights mapping |
| 1 | Some roles have defined authority; gaps are frequent; escalation is informal |
| 2 | Most significant decisions have mapped authority; some gaps; escalation exists but is inconsistently used |
| 3 | Comprehensive decision rights mapping; clear escalation paths; accountability auditable |
| 4 | Complete accountability architecture; human oversight explicitly designed for automated and AI-assisted decisions; tested under real conditions |

## Dimension 6: Institutional Resilience

**What is being measured:** The institution's demonstrated capacity to absorb disruption while preserving governance integrity, continuity, and legitimacy.

### Indicators

| Indicator | Questions to Answer |
|---|---|
| Recovery coherence | Did the institution maintain governance consistency during and after the last significant disruption? |
| Post-incident learning rate | What % of significant incidents produced documented governance updates? |
| Legitimacy stability | Did stakeholder trust remain stable during the last major transition or disruption? |
| Sovereignty posture | How operationally independent is the institution from critical single-vendor or individual dependencies? |

### Assessment Questions

1. What was the last significant institutional disruption (leadership change, crisis, major dispute, audit)? How did governance hold up?
2. How many of your most significant operational incidents in the last two years produced documented policy or governance changes?
3. During your last leadership transition, did you observe measurable change in member or stakeholder trust? What was the trajectory?
4. If your primary operational platform vendor became unavailable tomorrow, what governance function would you lose?

### Scoring Guide

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 0 | Institution is fragile; disruptions produce significant and sustained governance breakdown |
| 1 | Institution absorbs disruption but governance consistency suffers; slow recovery |
| 2 | Governance maintained through most disruptions; some learning from incidents; vendor dependencies unresolved |
| 3 | Demonstrable resilience; post-incident governance updates practiced; trust stability during transitions |
| 4 | Measured resilience improvement over time; learning loops institutionalized; sovereignty design deliberate and reviewed |

### Dimension Profile

Low scores on specific dimensions indicate where to invest first.

**If Memory Integrity < 2:** Knowledge risk audit is the immediate priority. Map critical dependencies before any other initiative.

**If Continuity Capacity < 2:** Transition planning is the immediate priority. A single leadership departure is a governance risk.

**If Governance Maturity < 2:** Governance architecture design is the priority. Decision traceability and policy ownership must be established before trust can be built.

**If Trust Operations < 2:** Explainability and challenge response processes are the priority. Trust cannot be claimed without operational evidence.

**If Accountability Architecture < 2:** Decision rights mapping is the priority. Accountability gaps make all other investments less durable.

**If Institutional Resilience < 2:** Dependency audit is the priority. Identify the failure modes that are one event away from becoming a crisis.
