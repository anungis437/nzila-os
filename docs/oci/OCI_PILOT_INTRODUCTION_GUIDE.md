# OCI Pilot Introduction Guide

**ARTIFACT TYPE:** Institutional Doctrine — Introduction Practice
**DOCTRINE_VERSION:** 1.0.0
**STATUS:** Canonical
**INTENDED USE:** Reference for the person making the first introduction of OCI to a prospective institutional sponsor — typically a sector partner, governance peer, or institutional intermediary
**PARENT FRAMEWORK:** [OCI Pilot Framework™](./OCI_PILOT_FRAMEWORK.md)

> An introduction is not a referral and is not a pitch. It is
> the moment one operator names a practice to another operator
> they know to be carrying the question. This guide names how
> that introduction is made well.

---

## What this document is

This document is a reference for the person making the first
introduction of OCI to a prospective institutional sponsor.

The introducer is typically:

- a sector partner who has worked with the prospective
  institution in another capacity,
- a governance peer who sits on a board with the prospective
  sponsor,
- a former colleague who recognises the continuity question is
  alive in the sponsor's institution,
- a member of the OCI facilitator network who has been asked,
  by a third party, to make an introduction.

The introducer is rarely a salesperson and is never a cold
outreach team. OCI is not introduced cold; it is introduced
between operators who already trust each other to be honest
about the institutional condition.

---

## Before the introduction

Three things should be true before an introduction is made.

### The institution is carrying the question

Recognition is the entry phase, and recognition must already be
forming inside the institution before an introduction will land.
Signals that recognition is forming:

- a recent senior departure that exposed continuity
  dependencies,
- a governance transition in progress or anticipated,
- a regulatory or member context in which continuity has
  surfaced,
- a sponsor's own observation, made in a conversation, that
  "our institution depends on a small number of people."

If none of these are present, the introduction is premature.
Wait for the question to surface.

### The sponsor is the right person to address

The sponsor must be able to:

- read the Executive Continuity Brief and decide,
- authorise the engagement on behalf of the institution,
- protect the engagement from premature operationalisation.

Common right-fit sponsors: chief executive officer, executive
director, secretary-general, general manager, president. A
mid-level operations leader is rarely the right sponsor; the
engagement requires institutional authority that an operations
leader typically does not carry alone.

### The introducer is willing to stand behind the introduction

An introduction is a small act of personal credit. The introducer
should be willing to remain in the conversation through the first
exchange, and should be willing for the sponsor to come back to
them with questions.

If the introducer is not willing to stand behind the
introduction, the introduction should not be made.

## The introduction itself

The introduction takes one of three forms, in descending order
of preference.

### Form 1 — In person

A short conversation, in person, between the introducer, the
prospective sponsor, and the facilitator. The introducer names
the facilitator, the work, and the reason for the introduction
in two or three sentences. The facilitator listens; the sponsor
asks what they wish to ask; the introducer steps back when the
conversation has its own footing.

This is the preferred form. It carries the most institutional
weight and the least extractive feel.

### Form 2 — Three-way written introduction

A short message from the introducer to the sponsor and the
facilitator, on a single thread, naming the introduction,
saying briefly why the work is being mentioned, and leaving the
next move to the sponsor. The facilitator does not write into
the thread until the sponsor has written back.

If the sponsor does not reply within two to three weeks, the
introducer follows up once, briefly. If no reply follows, the
introduction is set aside; the
[OCI Executive Email Sequence](./OCI_EXECUTIVE_EMAIL_SEQUENCE.md)
does not begin without the sponsor's first reply.

### Form 3 — Introduction by reference

The introducer mentions the work to the sponsor without
arranging a meeting and without naming the facilitator by email.
The sponsor takes the reference and decides, on their own time,
whether to ask the introducer for more.

This is the lightest form and the most respectful of the
sponsor's autonomy. It is appropriate when the sponsor is in a
period of institutional pressure or transition and would not
welcome a written thread.

## What the introducer says, and does not say

### What the introducer says

- The name of the work: "Operational Continuity Intelligence,
  or OCI."
- The institutional question: "It takes seriously the
  continuity load that institutions carry in a small number of
  people."
- Why the introduction: "It came to mind because of what you
  mentioned about [the institutional moment the sponsor named]."
- The facilitator's name and a one-sentence professional
  reference.
- The next-step posture: "I have offered to introduce you. The
  next move is yours, on your time."

### What the introducer does not say

- "You should hire them." The introducer recommends a
  conversation, not a contract.
- "They guarantee results." No such guarantee is made; the
  introducer does not invent one.
- "They are the best in the market." Comparative claims do not
  belong in the introduction.
- "They are AI-led" or "they have an AI platform." Neither is
  true; the work is reviewer-led. See
  [OCI AI Boundary](./OCI_AI_BOUNDARY.md).
- "They will help you transform / optimise / modernise / scale
  your institution." None of these words belong to the work.

## After the introduction

### If the sponsor engages

The conversation moves to the facilitator. The introducer is
told, briefly, that the conversation is continuing — no
substance is shared back, only the fact of continuation. The
introducer's role from this point is to be available if the
sponsor wishes to check a reference, and not to follow the
substance of the engagement.

### If the sponsor declines

The introducer thanks the sponsor for considering and closes
the thread. The facilitator is informed, briefly, that the
introduction did not proceed. No retry is attempted. No
re-pitch is made. If the sponsor's circumstances change later
and the question resurfaces, the sponsor knows where to find
the introducer; that is the only honest path.

### If the sponsor does not respond

After one follow-up by the introducer, the thread is closed.
Non-response is treated as a deliberate signal. The introducer
does not chase.

## Common mistakes

These mistakes are common enough to name explicitly. Each one
compromises the introduction.

- **Introducing the work as software.** The work is a
  methodology; the software is the runtime that supports it
  after the methodology has been chosen. Introducing software
  first inverts the doctrine.
- **Introducing without the question being present.** If the
  institution is not yet carrying the continuity question, the
  introduction lands as a sales touch and is correctly ignored.
- **Introducing to a non-sponsor.** A well-meant introduction
  to a director of operations who lacks institutional
  authority typically goes nowhere; worse, it can sour the
  later introduction to the right sponsor.
- **Introducing in a sales-deck idiom.** The introducer's
  voice should be the operator's voice, not the marketer's.
- **Introducing multiple practices at once.** OCI is the
  named practice. Other adjacent practices, if any, are named
  later by the sponsor's request, not by the introducer's
  bundling.

---

## A note on the network

OCI introductions accumulate slowly and intentionally. The
right rhythm for the network is fewer, deeper introductions
made by operators who have earned the standing to make them.
Volume is not the goal; correctness of fit is.

The work is built to be quietly recommended by people who have
seen it operate. Anything faster than that is faster than the
work itself.
